RESEARCH OVERVIEW & BENCHMARK DESIGN
How Reconstruction Errors Affect the FlyWire Connectome
Measuring how wiring diagram errors change network structure across 5 fruit fly connectomes
0:00 - 0:25 (25s)
Surjit Mandal
Why This Matters
Automated AI tools make mistakes when mapping brain wiring diagrams from electron microscope images.

This benchmark tests how much these errors change network connections, brain components, and neuron importance rankings.
Datasets Tested
• 5 Drosophila Connectomes: BANC (158k), FAFB (139k), MANC (24k), MCNS (167k), MAOL (52k neurons).
• 1,030 independent simulation runs across 10 error levels (0% to 20%).
• Tracked network connections, connected components, and PageRank rankings.
Dorkenwald et al. Nature 2024 Buhmann et al. Nat. Methods 2021 Januszewski et al. Nat. Methods 2018 Takemura et al. Nature 2023 Scheffer et al. eLife 2020
The Five Error Types Tested
1. Missed Synapses (EM1):
The detector fails to spot a real synaptic connection.
2. False Synapses (EM2):
The detector adds a fake connection where none exists.
3. Synapse Count Jitter (EM3):
Small counting errors in individual synapse numbers.
4. Split Neurons (EM4):
A single biological neuron gets broken into fragments.
5. Merged Neurons (EM5):
Two distinct neurons get accidentally joined together.
Main Goal
Provide clear sensitivity benchmarks showing how each error type affects brain graph measurements.

MISSED SYNAPSE ANALYSIS
Why Missed Synapses Do Not Proportionally Eliminate Connections
Connections with multiple synapses provide built-in protection against loss
1:00 - 1:40 (40s)
Synapse Loss vs Connection Loss
Evaluating whether losing 20% of synapses removes 20% of graph connections.
Main Results
• Total synapses drop by ~20.0%.
• Connection count drops by only: -4.87% (~4× smaller than synapse loss).
• Largest Connected Core (SCC) shrinks by only: -0.04%.
Why This Happens
In real connectomes, many connections have multiple synapses. A connection is only lost if every single synapse on it is missed (P = pw).

Under a 20% miss rate (illustrative example):
• 1-synapse edge: 20.0% chance of loss
• 5-synapse edge: 0.03% chance of loss

Connections with more synapses are strongly protected from disappearing.
Error model rates are motivated by automated synapse detection studies (Buhmann et al. 2021).
~4× Smaller Edge Loss: Total Synapses drops 1-to-1 while Edge Count is strongly protected.

Key Takeaway
Splitting redistributes existing connections across fragments while preserving total edge and synapse counts. Merging combines neurons, reducing edge count by 10.9% and increasing weight variance by 46.9%.
Splits and merges are important reconstruction error types in automated EM segmentation (Dorkenwald et al., 2024; Januszewski et al., 2018).

CENTRALITY & CONNECTOME COMPARISON
PageRank Remains Stable; Higher Connection Strength Is Associated With Lower Edge Loss
PageRank similarity to baseline remains high (r ≥ 0.977); edge loss varies with median connection weight
2:20 - 3:00 (40s)
Centrality & Wiring Resilience
Testing whether neuron importance rankings stay reliable and how observed edge loss varies across connectomes.
1. PageRank Similarity to Baseline Remains High
• PageRank remains highly correlated with the unperturbed baseline across the tested error models.
• Pearson correlations range from approximately 0.977 to 1.000 (mean r = 0.999 under missed synapses).
• PageRank rankings remain highly similar to the baseline under the tested perturbations.
2. Higher Connection Strength Is Associated With Lower Edge Loss
Under the 20% missed-synapse perturbation, observed edge loss varies across connectomes with different median connection weights:
• MCNS (median wt 9.0): -0.007% edge count change
• FAFB (median wt 6.0): -2.50% edge count change
• BANC (median wt 4.0): -3.19% edge count change
• MAOL (median wt 2.0): -8.92% edge count change
• MANC (median wt 2.0): -9.73% edge count change
Main Takeaway
PageRank remains highly similar to baseline under the tested perturbations, while connectomes with higher median connection weights show lower observed edge loss under the missed-synapse model.
Note: Observations reflect cross-connectome associations across the 5 tested datasets (Scheffer et al. eLife 2020).

SECONDARY ERROR FINGERPRINTS
Secondary Effects of Reconstruction Errors
Different reconstruction errors leave different fingerprints beyond the quantity directly perturbed.
3:00 - 3:30 (30s)
1. MISSED SYNAPSES
Direct: 20% of synapses are removed
SECONDARY EFFECT: Many individual synapses can be lost while most neuron-to-neuron connections remain.
Evidence: −20.0% synapses  |  −4.9% connections  |  PageRank r = 0.999
2. FALSE SYNAPSES
Direct: New false connections are added
SECONDARY EFFECT: Many new connections are added, but they are relatively weak, so connection count grows much faster than total synaptic strength.
Evidence: +19.4% connections  |  +7.6% synapses  |  −13.8% weight variance
3. SYNAPSE-COUNT NOISE
Direct: Connection weights changed; graph unchanged
SECONDARY EFFECT: The network structure stays the same, but connection strengths become less reliable.
Evidence: 0.0% connection change  |  0.0% degree/SCC  |  +5.5% weight variance
4. SPLIT ERRORS
Direct: Neurons split into multiple neuron pieces
SECONDARY EFFECT: Connections are kept, but they are spread across more neurons. This lowers connections per neuron and changes the largest connected group.
Evidence: +17.5% neurons  |  0.0% connection loss  |  −14.9% mean degree  |  +17.6% largest SCC
5. MERGE ERRORS
Direct: Multiple neurons combined into one
SECONDARY EFFECT: Most synapses remain, but merging neurons combines separate connections into fewer and much stronger-looking connections.
Evidence: −0.1% synapses  |  −10.9% connections  |  +46.9% weight variance  |  −9.0% largest SCC
KEY QUALITY-CONTROL TAKEAWAY
Each error changes the network in more ways than the direct error itself. Some errors mainly change connections, some change connection strength, and others change how connections are distributed across neurons.
Reliable connectome quality checks therefore need to look at several network measurements, not just one.
